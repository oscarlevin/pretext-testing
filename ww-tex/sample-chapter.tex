\begin{sectionptx}{Section}{The Quadratic Formula}{}{The Quadratic Formula}{}{}{section-the-quadratic-formula}
In the previous section, we saw relatively simple WeBWorK questions. This section demonstrates how even very complicated WeBWorK problems can still behave well.%
\par
Here is a theorem that gives us a formula for the solutions of a second-degree polynomial equation. Note later how the WeBWorK problem references the theorem by its number. This seemingly minor detail demonstrates the degree to which WeBWorK and PreTeXt have been integrated.%
\begin{theorem}{Theorem}{Quadratic Formula.}{}{theorem-quadratic-formula}%
Given the second-degree polynomial equation \(ax^2 + bx + c = 0\), where \(a\neq0\), solutions are given by%
\begin{equation*}
x = \frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}.
\end{equation*}
%
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}{Proof}{}{theorem-quadratic-formula-3}
%
\begin{align*}
ax^2 + bx + c &= 0\\
ax^2 + bx &= -c\\
4ax^2 + 4bx &= -4c\\
4ax^2 + 4bx + b^2 &= b^2 - 4ac\\
(2ax + b)^2 &= b^2 - 4ac\\
2ax + b &=\pm\sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}\\
2ax &=-b\pm\sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}\\
x &=\frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}
\end{align*}
%
\end{proof}

\begin{definition}{Definition}{Antiderivative of a Function.}{definition-antiderivative}%
Suppose that \(f(x)\) and \(F(x)\) are two functions such that%
\begin{equation*}
F^\prime(x) = f(x).
\end{equation*}
Then we say \(F\) is an \terminology{antiderivative} of \(f\).%
\end{definition}
The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus in one of the high points of a course in single-variable course.%
\begin{theorem}{Theorem}{The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus.}{}{theorem-FTC}%
\index{Fundamental Theorem of Calculus}%
If \(f(x)\) is continuous, and the derivative of \(F(x)\) is \(f(x)\), then%
\begin{equation*}
\definiteintegral{a}{b}{f(x)}{x}=F(b)-F(a)
\end{equation*}
%
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}{Proof}{}{theorem-FTC-4}
Left to the reader.%
\end{proof}
We state an equivalent version of the FTC, which is less-suited for computation, but which perhaps is a more interesting theoretical statement.%
\begin{corollary}{Corollary}{}{}{corollary-FTC-derivative}%
Suppose \(f(x)\) is a continuous function.  Then%
\begin{equation}
\frac{d}{dx}\definiteintegral{a}{x}{f(t)}{t}=f(x)\label{equation-alternate-FTC}
\end{equation}
%
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}{Proof}{}{proof-FTC-corollary}
We simply take the indicated derivative, applying \hyperref[theorem-FTC]{Theorem~{\xreffont\ref{theorem-FTC}}} at \hyperref[equation-use-FTC]{({\xreffont\ref{equation-use-FTC}})}.%
\begin{align}
\frac{d}{dx}\definiteintegral{a}{x}{f(t)}{t}&=\frac{d}{dx}\left(F(x)-F(a)\right)\label{equation-use-FTC}\\
&=\frac{d}{dx}F(x)-\frac{d}{dx}F(a)\notag\\
&=f(x)-0 = f(x)\label{proof-FTC-corollary-1-3-3}
\end{align}
%
\end{proof}
\end{subsectionptx}

Tabulars can be made using the existing syntax and options that PTX offers for table-making, with some exceptions. As of summer 2017, the exceptions are that individual cells cannot have \mono{bottom} border attributes and individual columns cannot have \mono{top} border attributes. \noindent\textbf{\blocktitlefont Solution}.\hypertarget{cylinder-volume-5-back}{}\quad{}We use \(r\) to represent the base's radius, and \(h\) to represent the cylinder's height.%
\par
A cylinder's volume formula is \(V= (\text{base area}) \cdot \text{height}\). A cylinder's base is a circle, with its area formula \(A = \pi r^{2}\).%
\par
Putting together these two formulas, we have a cylinder's volume formula:%
\par
\(\displaystyle{ V= \pi r^{2} h }\)%
\par
Throughout these computations, all quantities have units attached, and we only show them in the final step.%
\par
%
\begin{enumerate}[label={\alph*.}]
\item{}Using the volume formula, we have:%
\par
\(\displaystyle{\begin{aligned}
V \amp  = \pi r^{2} h \\
\amp  = \pi \cdot 6^{2} \cdot 10 \\
\amp  = \pi \cdot 360 \\
\amp  = 360 \pi \textrm{ m}^3
\end{aligned}}\)%
\par
Don't forget the volume unit \(\textrm{m}^3\).%
\item{}To find the decimal version, we replace \(\pi\) with its decimal value, and we have:%
\par
\(\displaystyle{\begin{aligned}[t]
V\amp  = 360 \pi \\
\amp  \approx 360 \cdot 3.14\ldots \\
\amp  \approx {1130.97\ {\rm m^{3}}}
\end{aligned}}\)%
\par
Don't forget the volume unit \(\textrm{m}^3\).%
\end{enumerate}
%
\end{inlinesolution}%
